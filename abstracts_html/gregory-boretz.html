    Poster
    Meta-Awareness During Naturalistic High-Arousal Events: A Longitudinal N-of-1 Phenomenological Case Study

    Background Consciousness research frequently prioritizes third-person mechanisms while treating first-person reports as secondary data. This paper examines whether meta-aware observation can remain available during intense autonomic, affective, and conditioned reactivity. The Avatar–Conductor distinction is used as a descriptive phenomenological heuristic: “Avatar” denotes automatic embodied and conditioned responses, while “Conductor” denotes the experienced capacity to notice those responses without immediate identification or enactment. Method This N-of-1 autoethnographic case study draws on longitudinal written records and structured reconstructions of episodes involving family conflict, bereavement, and other naturalistic high-arousal events. A preliminary qualitative analysis organizes these episodes around six experiential features: precipitating event, bodily activation, affective impulse, narrative identification, emergence of observing awareness, and subsequent attentional or behavioral change. Other individuals appear only as de-identified contextual features of the author’s lived environment; they are not treated as research participants, and no clinical claims are made about them. Findings Preliminary observations suggest that meta-aware observation does not prevent autonomic activation. Instead, it may alter the individual’s relationship to that activation, producing experiential differences in identification, action selection, and recovery trajectory. The distinction appears most consequential when observing awareness remains available while the reactive system is strongly activated. Conclusion The Avatar–Conductor distinction is not presented as evidence of a metaphysically separate self or as a competing neural theory of consciousness. It is proposed as a first-person vocabulary and observational framework that may complement mechanism-first accounts and inform future neurophenomenological, microphenomenological, or experience-sampling research. Limitations include retrospective bias, single-subject generalizability, and the need for prospective replication.

    About the speaker

    Greg Boretz is founder of The Undersong Project and SoloBoss LLC, working at the intersection of business strategy, AI, and human development. His background spans early online education (co-developing AI-driven learning models in the 1990s), startup consulting, and capital raising. He has served on the board of the Inattentive ADHD Coalition and writes on embodiment, attention, and consciousness.
