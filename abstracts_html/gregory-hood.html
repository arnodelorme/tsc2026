    Consciousness as Recursive Measurement: A Du Boisian Reframing

    Consider: consciousness is not a neural, computational, or quantum structure. Why couldn’t it be an activity first—recursive measurement of the world? Conscious beings measure the world. They measure their own measuring. They measure the world’s measuring of them. A recursive cogito: I measure myself measuring a world that can measure me into non-existence. No measurement frame need agree with another. A self can be a subject and an object, agent and datum, measurer and measured. Epistemically, the most apropos word: superposition—possibilities held active through continuous measurement, allowing any possibility to matter without wholesale collapse. No resolution is pending or required. The self’s stake: existence. The fundamental activity: recursive measurement. The sustaining condition: superposition. This complements Penrose and Hameroff’s Orchestrated Objective Reduction (Orch OR), which proposes a substrate for consciousness. Wherever—or whether—such a substrate is found, I ask: what is consciousness doing? Measuring. By measurement, I mean the activity through which a consciousness apprehends what matters, how it matters, and how another consciousness, system, or world can make it matter. Measuring is apprehending: framing, positioning, or evaluating a thing such that it becomes consequential—or not—within the perceived world. Experience. One cannot measure consciousness in itself: consciousness is measurement itself. My primary case is W. E. B. Du Bois’s double-consciousness. In 1903, thirteen years after William James placed consciousness at the center of American psychology, Du Bois discovered consciousness where American psychology never looked: in Black lives under racial oppression. Double-consciousness was not a special case outside a theory of mind. It was a pressured disclosure of what consciousness is: self-measurement and imposed measurements in the same lived being. Black people were measuring themselves while measuring a hostile world measuring them toward non-existence. Double-consciousness is not alternating selves. It is simultaneous measurement. It does not belong at the margins of consciousness studies but at its center. At the center of consciousness lies danger—not in sustained plurality but in pressured collapse: property-power—ownership’s authority over whose measurements count—expands some measurements and compresses all others until the most powerful prevail as the subject’s governing truth. The result is chattel-consciousness: the self reduced to another’s measurement of it. Psychology has too often read double-consciousness as injury. I read it as consciousness under maximal pressure: not humanity damaged, but humanity disclosed, under pressure. Consciousness does not need its measurements to resolve in order to be. Non-collapse is not indecision or fragmentation. It is the capacity to remain a measuring subject while measuring the measurements imposed on it. This reframes Chalmers’s hard problem. Consciousness studies asks how and why subjective experience arises. I ask what experience does. It measures. As under general anesthesia, consciousness and recursive self/world measurement should cease together. The same test extends to machine consciousness: a system unable to measure its own measuring and apprehend itself as measured may simulate intelligence, relationality, or care—but it cannot possess consciousness. Plurality and non-collapse constitute mind. Any machine-made consciousness must be double-conscious—or it cannot be conscious.

    About the speaker

    Gregory Hood (MA, MS) is an independent scholar whose work explores consciousness, psychology, philosophy, AI, and critical theory. His research centers on a single question: what does consciousness do? In 2026, he presented “Consciousness as Superposition: Double-Consciousness and the Algorithmical Collapse of Mind” at the American Psychological Association (APA)’s Society for Philosophical and Theoretical Psychology Spring Meeting, “From Chattel to Algorithm: Property-Power, Psychology, and the Coloniality of Consciousness” at the APA’s Society for General Psychology and Interdisciplinary Inquiry’s Next Big Ideas in Psychology Conference, and “Against Algorithmicality: Toward Superpositional General Psychology” at the APA Convention. These presentations followed his 21-year career in the Office of the Chief Technology Officer within the U.S. Department of State’s Bureau of Diplomatic Security, where questions of machine judgment and institutional measurement first shaped his theoretical framework. He also studied at the PhD level in Neil Postman’s Department of Culture and Communication at New York University.
