    The Color Humans Cannot See. Which Theories of Consciousness Survive the Teleportation Test?

    A dog standing in a field of brilliant red flowers does not see red. The color exists; the dog simply lacks the biological machinery to perceive it. This paper asks whether teleportation occupies a similar place in human cognition. Rather than assuming teleportation is impossible, we ask whether it represents a conceptual "color" that current scientific understanding cannot yet perceive. If so, teleportation becomes more than a technological speculation. It becomes a powerful tool for examining the nature of consciousness itself. Teleportation has traditionally been discussed either as a problem in physics or as a staple of science fiction. This paper proposes a different perspective: teleportation as a conceptual stress test for theories of consciousness. Regardless of whether physical teleportation is ultimately achievable, asking what exactly would be transported exposes unresolved questions concerning subjective experience, personal identity, and the relationship between matter and information. If every atom of a human body were reconstructed while preserving its complete informational state, would the resulting individual remain the same conscious observer or merely become an indistinguishable copy? If consciousness depends upon uninterrupted physical continuity, what preserves personal identity during normal biological processes in which much of the body's molecular composition is naturally replaced over time? Rather than asking whether teleportation is possible, this paper asks a different question: Which current theories of consciousness survive the teleportation test? Teleportation is treated not as an engineering proposal but as a common benchmark against which competing theories can be systematically compared. The analysis examines predictions made by Global Workspace Theory, Integrated Information Theory, predictive processing, Higher-Order Thought theories, and the quantum-based Orchestrated Objective Reduction. Each framework is evaluated according to its predictions regarding continuity of subjective experience, duplication, informational identity, and the persistence of the conscious self. The paper further explores whether intuitions about teleportation and personal identity vary across generations shaped by different technological environments. Individuals whose formative years occurred in an analog world may place greater emphasis on biological continuity and physical embodiment. In contrast, individuals who matured in an era of persistent digital identities, cloud computing, virtual environments, and artificial intelligence may be more willing to view informational continuity as sufficient for the persistence of the self. Rather than assuming a simple age effect, this paper proposes that lived technological experience may shape intuitive models of consciousness, offering a testable hypothesis at the intersection of consciousness science, cognitive psychology, and human-computer interaction. Drawing upon the work of Derek Parfit and David Chalmers, the paper argues that teleportation is valuable not because it predicts future technology, but because it exposes hidden assumptions within existing theories of consciousness. Like Maxwell's Demon or Schrödinger's Cat, the teleportation test functions as a rigorous conceptual framework that reveals where theories converge, diverge, or fail to generate falsifiable predictions. By forcing explicit definitions of what constitutes the persistence of a conscious self, teleportation provides a unified benchmark for advancing interdisciplinary research across consciousness science, neuroscience, artificial intelligence, quantum information, and the philosophy of mind.

    About the speaker

    Olga Diamandis is an independent researcher exploring the intersection of consciousness, identity, artificial intelligence, and emerging technologies. Her work examines how provocative thought experiments can illuminate fundamental questions about subjective experience, personal identity, and the limits of human perception. Drawing from cognitive science, neuroscience, quantum information, philosophy of mind, and innovation research, she investigates how transformative technologies challenge long-held assumptions about what it means to be human.

    Rather than asking whether technologies such as teleportation are immediately achievable, Olga studies them as scientific frameworks for examining continuity of consciousness, the relationship between information and identity, and the distinction between physical replication and subjective experience. Her current research explores how these questions may reshape our understanding of consciousness in an era increasingly defined by artificial intelligence and digital representations of the self.

    Olga is the Founder and CEO of MasterVortex, an innovation research organization dedicated to exploring the long-term implications of disruptive technologies. Over the past two decades, she has led innovation and strategic foresight initiatives for Fortune 500 organizations across healthcare, technology, telecommunications, and consumer industries. She is the author of But What If I'm Not Elon Musk?! and is currently writing a series of works examining organizational consciousness, human-AI collaboration, and the future of transformative innovation.

    At Consciousness Science 2026, she presents a conceptual framework proposing teleportation as a rigorous thought experiment for investigating continuity of consciousness, personal identity, and the epistemological limits of human perception.
