    Transformations of Consciousness During Prolonged Uncertainty: A Phenomenological Autoethnography of the Doha Airspace Crisis

    Acute geopolitical crises disrupt not only physical safety and everyday routines but also the structure of conscious experience. While psychological research has extensively documented stress, anxiety, and trauma associated with crises, comparatively little attention has been paid to how consciousness itself changes as individuals navigate prolonged uncertainty. First-person methodologies offer a unique opportunity to examine these transformations directly. This study presents a phenomenological autoethnography of lived experience during the 2026 Doha airspace crisis, during which the author became stranded following the sudden closure of regional airspace while travelling to an academic conference in Europe. The study draws upon a rich corpus of reflexive audio recordings produced in the weeks following the experience, contemporaneous observations, travel documents, photographs, personal communications, and subsequent phenomenological reflection. Rather than reconstructing a chronological account of events, the analysis explores transformations in the lived structure of consciousness across the unfolding crisis. A phenomenological autoethnographic approach was employed, combining systematic first-person reflection with thematic interpretation to examine how experience evolved from the disruption of ordinary expectations through prolonged uncertainty to post-event integration. Preliminary analysis suggests that the experience was characterised by a progressive reorganisation of conscious experience rather than psychological distress alone. Emerging experiential structures include the collapse of predictability; altered experiences of temporality, spatiality, and embodiment; heightened awareness of interpersonal relationships; shifts in trust, social identity, and perceived human connectedness; changing perceptions of self and agency; and evolving processes of existential reflection and meaning-making. Familiar environments acquired new experiential significance, ordinary social encounters became sources of existential reassurance, and episodes of flow and mindfulness emerged as adaptive modes of consciousness amidst uncertainty. The subsequent narration and interpretation of these experiences further transformed their perceived significance, illustrating the dynamic relationship between lived experience, memory, and meaning. This paper argues that prolonged uncertainty may be understood as a reorganisation of consciousness involving interconnected changes in perception, temporality, embodiment, sociality, and selfhood. Beyond documenting an individual experience, the study demonstrates how rigorous phenomenological autoethnography can generate theoretically relevant insights for consciousness science while complementing third-person empirical approaches. The work also reflects on how this lived experience became the catalyst for a broader programme of phenomenological and qualitative research on consciousness under conditions of existential uncertainty. By positioning first-person inquiry as a legitimate source of scientific insight, the study contributes to ongoing discussions on phenomenology, autoethnography, and the integration of first- and third-person approaches in consciousness research.

    About the speaker

    Dr. Maria Fortuna Patrick, MD (Physiology), MBBS, PGDip (Philosophy), is an interdisciplinary researcher and Assistant Professor of Physiology at PSG Institute of Medical Sciences & Research, India. Her work explores subjective experience, meta-awareness, meaning-making, and psychological well-being using qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods approaches. Her current research includes phenomenological studies of crisis experience and investigations of contemplative practices, self-transcendence, and decentering in medical populations. She has research exposure at NIMHANS, Bengaluru, and is particularly interested in phenomenology, consciousness studies, social psychology, and the experiential dimensions of mental health and human adaptation.
