    Poster
    Space, Time, Causation, and (AI) Consciousness: Brains are Analog Because Coherently Constructing an Integrated Qualitative World-Model at any Present Instant Requires It

    This paper argues that the existence and nature of consciousness are best explained by manifest asymmetries between space and time. I show that because the present manifests as immeasurably short in duration, to reason effectively about its environment in real-time a cognitive system must (i) construct integrated qualitative world-models, (ii) distributed across space, (iii) at every present instant, (iv) updating them accurately and coherently across time. I then show that this explains why brains systematically utilize analog computation, why consciousness appears so confounding, and why digital computers probably cannot realize consciousness or intentional mental states (beliefs, desires, hopes, fears, etc.) that causally impact their behavior. First, given the ephemeral nature of the present, to reason effectively about its environment a system must model the world as it is now, representing various qualities of the present state of the world (colors, sounds, shapes, etc.) simultaneously as an integrated whole. Second, only analog computation can achieve this. Whereas digital computers consist only of unintegrated binary (“on/off”) representations at any present instant, analog computation can utilize graded magnitudes of diverse phenomena (such as different levels of electrical activation) to simultaneously represent different qualities in the world at a single moment, instantaneously integrating them into a unified world-model distributed across space. Third, consciousness appears to be so philosophically and scientifically mysterious because, to fit such qualitative representations into an irreducibly short present, brains must compress them into maximally simple units (i.e. individual color-qualities, sounds, etc.), which thus appear to any system which has them to resist any further (scientific or functional) explanation as an apparently-irreducible “subjective point-of-view.” Finally, insofar as intentional mental states manifest as depending upon conscious experience, insofar as causation manifests as a relationship between occurrent events, and insofar as events manifest as occurring at the present, it follows that only analog computing devices (e.g., brains) can realize consciousness, intentional mental states, and use such states to cause their future behavior.

    About the speaker

    Marcus Arvan is Professor of Philosophy and Chair of Philosophy and Religion at the University of Tampa. He received his PhD from the University of Arizona and works in ethics, social-political philosophy, cognitive science, philosophy of AI, philosophy of mind, and metaphysics.

    He has published three books: Why It's OK to be a Moderate (2025), Neurofunctional Prudence and Morality: A Philosophical Theory (2020), and Rightness as Fairness: A Moral and Political Theory (2016).
