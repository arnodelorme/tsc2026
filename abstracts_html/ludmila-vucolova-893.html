    Functional Similarities in the Emergence of Subjective Awareness: Parallels Between Neuroscience and Quantum Physics

    Understanding how subjective awareness emerges from physical processes remains one of the central challenges in neuroscience and consciousness research. Although neuroscience and quantum physics investigate different levels of physical organization, both confront the problem of how multiple interacting states become integrated into unified outcomes. This paper argues that these disciplines share two important conceptual principles relevant to consciousness: (1) entanglement or the binding of initially distinct states into an inseparable whole, and (2) state transitions associated with changes in underlying space-time organization. I present the Collapse into Translation theory, which proposes that consciousness emerges through the collapse of first-order perceptual states and higher-order symbolic states during embodied sensory-motor interaction with the environment. Rather than identifying consciousness with perception alone or with higher-order cognition alone, the theory proposes that subjective awareness arises from the dynamic integration of both. This process enables the translation of physical stimuli into symbolic representations, thereby generating conscious experience. Formally, the theory is expressed as C = (X ≡ Y), where X* denotes a first-order perceptual state and Y* denotes a higher-order symbolic state. Consciousness emerges when these initially distinct states become functionally unified into a single relational state (X ≡ Y). The resulting conscious state cannot be fully described by either component independently but exists as an integrated system in which phenomenal properties of perception become attributed to symbolic representation. This collapse produces two complementary physical expressions of a unified mental state while preserving their functional interdependence. The theory identifies three structural variables governing conscious experience: S1, the primary sensory modality receiving external stimulation; M, the motor system generating self-initiated symbolic action (vocal articulation or manual signing); and S2, the sensory modality registering the consequences of that action. Consciousness therefore depends not on any particular sensory modality but on the relational organization linking perception, action, and sensory feedback. This framework accommodates diverse forms of embodiment, including blindness, deafness, sign language, and deaf-blind communication, while preserving the same underlying principle. The proposed collapse exhibits functional similarities to quantum mechanical descriptions in which multiple potential states become integrated into a single realized state. Likewise, the production of symbolic actions through vocal or manual movement requires continuous physical reconfiguration in space and time. These observations suggest conceptual parallels between embodied neural dynamics and proposals in quantum theory that associate state reduction with changes in space-time geometry, including the framework proposed by Sir Roger Penrose. The present theory does not claim that quantum processes directly generate consciousness; rather, it identifies analogous principles of state integration and structural transformation operating across distinct scientific domains. The Collapse into Translation framework generates experimentally testable predictions concerning sensory-motor coupling, symbolic processing, and the transition from unconscious processing to conscious awareness. By identifying a common organizational principle underlying diverse forms of conscious experience, the theory provides a unified framework integrating neuroscience, cognitive science, philosophy of mind, computational modeling, and artificial intelligence. More broadly, it offers a novel perspective on how subjective awareness may emerge through the translation of embodied physical interactions into symbolic mental representations

    About the speaker

    I was educated in Russia, graduating from the Electro-technical University, Saint Petersburg, Soviet Union, holding an MS in telemechanics and remote-control engineering. My experience includes 40 years of research, with two inventions in electrical engineering and 29 copyrighted hypotheses across multiple areas of natural science: evolution of humans, evolution of language, functional asymmetry and specialization of the brain, mirror neurons, Parkinson’s disease, and consciousness.

    My hypothesis How a strictly physical universe gave rise to consciousness has been recommended for testing by Ken Wang, Program Director, Information and Intelligent Systems Division and Jonathan Fritz, Program Manager, Cognitive Neuroscience Program (National Science Foundation).
