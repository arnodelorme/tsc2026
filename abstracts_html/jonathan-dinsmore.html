    The structure of human conscious experience beyond the body: a large-scale phenomenological analysis of OBE narratives
    Marina Weiler, Jonathan Dinsmore, Phillip Cozzolino
    Marina Weiler — University of Virginia, Charlottesville, VA, USA
    Jonathan Dinsmore — University of Arkansas, Fayetteville, AR, USA
    Phillip Cozzolino — University of Virginia, Charlottesville, VA, USA

    The sense of being located within one’s physical body is central to ordinary conscious experience, yet some individuals report episodes in which awareness appears displaced from the body, commonly known as out-of-body experiences (OBEs). Although neuroscientific accounts often emphasize autoscopy and floating above the body, first-person reports indicate a substantially broader phenomenological range. This study examined 1,064 first-person OBE narratives, representing, to the authors’ knowledge, the largest reported qualitative analysis of OBE narratives to date. Narratives were deductively coded using a large language model anchored to a codebook comprising 12 domain themes and 32 phenomenological subthemes. Coding was validated against consensus human ratings of 70 randomly selected reports, yielding 83.1% overall agreement. Common features included physical surroundings (84.1%), floating or hovering (55.5%), autoscopy (54.5%), encounters with living people (53.6%), and meaning-making (52.7%). Transcendent features were also frequent, including light experiences (36.4%), divine entities (19.8%), and spiritual realms (18.7%). Co-occurrence and latent-structure analyses partially supported a dimensional model, with a primary axis ranging from near-body, perceptually continuous experiences to far or transcendent experiences, together with a second axis ranging from human-like to formless embodiment. Three groups were derived directly from each report’s coded phenomenology: stayed-near experiences without spiritual or cosmic destination content (n = 773, 72.7%), far-dominant experiences containing a distant destination but little or no near-body stage (n = 129, 12.1%), and full-arc experiences containing both near-body and distant-destination content (n = 162, 15.2%). Beyond the features used to construct the groups, far-dominant and full-arc reports showed substantially more meaning-making, light experiences, and encounters with divine entities than stayed-near reports, whereas flying was most characteristic of full-arc experiences. Somatic onset phenomena and some modes of locomotion showed less consistent alignment with the primary axes, suggesting that they may form cross-cutting aspects of OBE phenomenology. These findings show that OBE phenomenology extends well beyond autoscopy and is better characterized as a heterogeneous, multidimensional family of experiences with primary axes and provisional categorical groupings.

    About the speaker

    Jonathan Dinsmore is a psychological researcher whose work focuses on the empirical phenomenology of anomalous and transformative experiences. His research includes psychometric scale development, as well as mixed methods approaches that integrate qualitative research into scientific investigation to examine how unusual experiences are structured, interpreted, and related to psychological outcomes. He also conducts theoretical work aimed at clarifying contested constructs, improving conceptual precision, and developing methodologically rigorous frameworks for the scientific study of consciousness and exceptional human experience.
